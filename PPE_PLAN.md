# Retia: PPE Content Plan (Draft)

> Politics, Philosophy and Economics. Draft plan for the first year of Folios. Last updated: 2026-09-29

**Format reminder:** 1 Folio = 15 days × 7 notes = **105 notes**. 2 Folios a month = **24 Folios a year**.

---

## Structure: all three subjects every day (decided)

- Every day has **7 notes mixing all three subjects**, split **3 / 2 / 2** and rotating which subject gets 3:
  - Day I: Philosophy 3 · Economics 2 · Politics 2
  - Day II: Philosophy 2 · Economics 3 · Politics 2
  - Day III: Philosophy 2 · Economics 2 · Politics 3
  - …and repeat. Over 15 days each subject gets exactly **35 notes per Folio**
- Each subject follows its own **theme** (a "strand"). A theme is ~105 notes, so it runs across **3 Folios (~6 weeks)**, with all three strands moving forward side by side
- Notes of the same subject sit together within a day (Philosophy notes, then Economics, then Politics) so it doesn't jump around mid-thought

---

## How to write a note (bite-sized, for a normal person on the bus)

Not just a definition. Each note = **what it is + how it works + a real example + why it matters**, in plain words. (v2: more detail than the first draft.)

- **In short:** one sentence, the core idea
- **Explained:** 2–4 sentences on how it works: the nuance, the types, a common confusion, or who came up with it
- **Example:** a concrete everyday or real-world case, ideally one that shows the nuance (a contrast, a flip side, a hidden part)
- **Why it matters:** one or two lines on where you'll run into it or how to use it
- Length: about **110–150 words**, around a minute to read. May need a small scroll on a phone; the Understood button stays pinned at the bottom
- Title = the thing itself ("Opportunity cost"), because the Recollection round asks "What is… opportunity cost?"

Template:
```

**Explained:** Costs aren't only money. Time, effort and missed chances count too. Economists look at the *next best* option you didn't take, because that's what the choice really cost you. Two people can pay the same price for something and face very different opportunity costs.
**Example:** ...
```

## Philosophy strands

**1 · Thinking tools**: arguments (premise, conclusion, valid, sound), deduction vs induction, logical fallacies, cognitive biases, burden of proof, Occam's razor, falsifiability, steelmanning, thought experiments, the Socratic method

**2 · Ancient thinkers**: Pre-Socratics, Socrates, Plato (Forms, the Cave, the Republic), Aristotle (virtue, golden mean, logic), Stoics (Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, Seneca), Epicureans, Cynics & Sceptics, Confucius, Laozi, the Buddha

**3 · Ethics**: consequentialism & utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill), deontology (Kant, categorical imperative), virtue ethics, contractualism, moral relativism vs realism, trolley problems, moral luck, egoism & altruism

**4 · Knowledge and mind**: what is knowledge (justified true belief, Gettier), rationalism vs empiricism (Descartes, Locke, Hume), scepticism, the problem of induction, mind–body problem, dualism vs physicalism, consciousness & qualia, personal identity

**5 · Political philosophy**: state of nature, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Mill's harm principle, positive vs negative liberty, Rawls (veil of ignorance), Nozick, Marx, communitarianism, rights, justice & equality

**6 · Modern philosophy**: Kant, Hegel (dialectic), Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, Kierkegaard, existentialism (Sartre, de Beauvoir), absurdism (Camus), pragmatism (James, Dewey)

**7 · 20th century**: Russell & Frege, Wittgenstein, logical positivism, Popper (falsification), Kuhn (paradigm shifts), Arendt, Foucault (power/knowledge), postmodernism, feminist philosophy

**8 · Big questions**: free will vs determinism, meaning of life, death, personal identity puzzles, animal ethics, effective altruism, ethics of AI, philosophy of technology

## Economics strands

**1 · Thinking like an economist**: scarcity, opportunity cost, incentives, marginal thinking, supply & demand, price signals, elasticity, trade-offs, comparative advantage, sunk costs, unintended consequences

**2 · Microeconomics**: consumer choice & utility, firms & costs, perfect competition, monopoly & oligopoly, price discrimination, market failure, externalities, public goods, tragedy of the commons, information asymmetry

**3 · Macroeconomics**: GDP, growth, inflation & deflation, unemployment, business cycles, recessions, fiscal policy, monetary policy, central banks, government debt, exchange rates

**4 · Money, banking and crises**: what money is, fiat vs commodity money, how banks create money, interest rates, bonds & stocks (as concepts, not advice), bubbles (tulips, South Sea, dot-com), 1929, 2008, hyperinflation (Weimar, Zimbabwe)

**5 · History of economic thought**: mercantilism, Adam Smith (invisible hand), Ricardo, Malthus, Marx, marginal revolution, Keynes, Hayek & the Austrian school, Friedman & monetarism, Schumpeter (creative destruction)

**6 · Behavioural economics and game theory**: bounded rationality, loss aversion, prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky), nudges, prisoner's dilemma, Nash equilibrium, coordination games, signalling, auctions

**7 · Development and trade**: why nations are rich or poor, institutions (Acemoglu & Robinson), the Industrial Revolution, East Asian growth miracles, globalisation, tariffs & trade wars, foreign aid debate, the resource curse

**8 · Public economics and inequality**: taxation (progressive, flat, VAT), welfare state, inequality measures (Gini), Piketty's r > g, universal basic income, healthcare economics, climate economics (carbon tax, cap-and-trade)

## Politics strands

**1 · Core concepts**: power (Weber, Lukes' three faces), the state, sovereignty, legitimacy (traditional, charismatic, legal-rational), authority, nation vs state, citizenship, rule of law, separation of powers

**2 · Political systems**: democracy (direct vs representative), presidential vs parliamentary, electoral systems (FPTP, PR), federal vs unitary, constitutions, judicial review, authoritarianism & totalitarianism, hybrid regimes, one-party states

**3 · Ideologies**: liberalism, conservatism, socialism, social democracy, communism, libertarianism, nationalism, fascism, anarchism, populism, green politics, the left–right spectrum

**4 · Political history**: Athenian democracy, the Roman Republic, Magna Carta, the Glorious Revolution, American & French Revolutions, 1848, the rise of mass politics, the World Wars, decolonisation, the Cold War, 1989

**5 · International relations**: realism, liberalism, constructivism, balance of power, security dilemma, nuclear deterrence, the UN, NATO, the EU, soft vs hard power, globalisation, great-power rivalry

**6 · Comparative politics**: how the US, UK, France, Germany, China, Russia, India and Japan are governed, and why their systems differ

**7 · Persuasion, rhetoric and propaganda**: Aristotle's ethos / pathos / logos, classical rhetoric, Cicero, Machiavelli, propaganda techniques, framing & agenda-setting, political speeches, campaigns, polling, media & misinformation, Cialdini's principles of persuasion

**8 · Public policy and today's issues**: how policy is made, bureaucracy, lobbying & interest groups, immigration, climate politics, tech & AI governance, surveillance & privacy, the future of democracy

## Sample: Folio I, Day I (Philosophy 3 · Economics 2 · Politics 2)

```
# Folio I
Philosophy: Thinking tools
Economics: Thinking like an economist
Politics: Core concepts

## Day I

### Philosophy | Argument vs opinion
**In short:** An opinion is a claim. An argument is a claim backed by reasons meant to support it.
**Explained:** In philosophy, "argument" doesn't mean a shouting match; it means giving reasons. Opinions aren't bad, and everyone has them, but on their own they can't be checked. Once reasons are attached, other people can examine them: are the reasons true, and do they actually support the claim?
**Example:** "Pineapple on pizza is gross" is an opinion. "Pineapple on pizza is a bad idea because the juice makes the base soggy" is an argument. Now someone can reply, "Not if you bake it longer," and you're actually debating instead of just disagreeing.
**Why it matters:** When someone just repeats an opinion louder, ask "why?" If no reason comes back, there's nothing there to be persuaded by.

### Philosophy | Premises and conclusion
**In short:** Premises are the reasons; the conclusion is what they're meant to prove.
**Explained:** Every argument has at least one premise and one conclusion. Signal words help: "because", "since" and "given that" usually introduce premises; "so", "therefore" and "which means" introduce the conclusion. In real life the conclusion often comes first, and some premises are left unsaid. These are called hidden premises.
**Example:** "Don't buy that car, it's a diesel." Conclusion: don't buy it. Stated premise: it's a diesel. Hidden premise: diesel cars are a bad buy (fuel prices, city bans). The hidden premise is the part actually worth questioning.
**Why it matters:** The conclusion tells you what someone wants you to believe. The hidden premises show you where the argument is weakest.

### Philosophy | Valid vs sound
**In short:** Valid means the conclusion follows from the premises. Sound means it's valid *and* the premises are true.
**Explained:** Validity is about the shape of an argument, not the facts. Ask: if the premises were true, would the conclusion have to be true? If yes, it's valid, even if the premises are nonsense. Soundness adds the reality check. Only a sound argument guarantees a true conclusion.
**Example:** "All fish can fly. A salmon is a fish. So salmon can fly." Valid, but the first premise is false, so it's unsound. The flip side: "Some politicians lie. Bob is a politician. So Bob lies." The premises may well be true, but the conclusion doesn't follow. Invalid.
**Why it matters:** Arguments fail in two ways: bad logic or bad facts. Check both before you accept the conclusion.

### Economics | Scarcity
**In short:** Resources are limited but wants aren't, so choices have to be made.
**Explained:** Scarcity doesn't mean something is rare. It means there isn't enough for everyone to have as much as they want for free. Time, money, land, workers and attention are all scarce. So every person, business and government has to decide what to do, what to give up, and who gets what. Economics is basically the study of those choices.
**Example:** A hospital has a fixed budget and a limited number of doctors. Opening a new cancer ward might mean longer waits in the emergency room. Neither option is "free", even if the patient never sees a bill.
**Why it matters:** When a politician promises something with no downside, scarcity is the question to ask: what gets less so this can get more?

### Economics | Opportunity cost
**In short:** The real cost of something is the best alternative you gave up to get it.
**Explained:** Costs aren't only money. Time, effort and missed chances count too. Economists look at the *next best* option you didn't take, because that's what the choice really cost you. Two people can pay the same price for something and face very different opportunity costs.
**Example:** University costs tuition, but also the wages you could have earned working for those 3–4 years. For most people it's still worth it. For a talented young footballer, or a teenager whose business is taking off, the hidden cost can be huge, which is why some sensibly skip it.
**Why it matters:** Price tags hide the biggest costs. Asking "what am I giving up?" makes decisions, personal and political, much clearer.

### Politics | Power
**In short:** Power is the ability to get others to do what they otherwise wouldn't.
**Explained:** Power comes in several forms. Coercion uses force or threats. Reward uses money, jobs or favours. Authority works when people accept that someone has the *right* to give orders. The political theorist Steven Lukes added a quieter kind: shaping what people want, or think is even possible, so they never object in the first place.
**Example:** A boss can order you to stay late (authority), pay you overtime (reward), threaten your job (coercion), or build a culture where staying late is "just what committed people do" (shaping beliefs). The last one doesn't need an order at all.
**Why it matters:** Politics is largely about who holds power, how they got it, and whether they use it fairly. The quiet kind is the hardest to notice, and the most useful to spot.

### Politics | The state
**In short:** The state is the permanent machinery that makes and enforces rules over a territory.
**Explained:** The state includes the government, parliament, courts, police, army and civil service. It's different from *the government*, which is just the group running it at the moment. The sociologist Max Weber defined the state as the organisation that claims the only *legitimate* right to use force in a territory. Others can use force, but only the state's use is accepted as rightful.
**Example:** After an election the government changes, but the tax office, courts and army carry on. And if a private company locks you in a room, that's kidnapping; if the police detain you under the law, it's legitimate.
**Why it matters:** Arguments about "big" versus "small" government are really arguments about how much the state should do.
```
