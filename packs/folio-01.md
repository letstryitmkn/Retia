# Folio I
Philosophy: Thinking tools
Economics: Thinking like an economist
Politics: Core concepts

## Day I

### Philosophy | Argument vs opinion
**In short:** An opinion is a claim. An argument is a claim backed by reasons meant to support it.
**Explained:** In philosophy, "argument" doesn't mean a shouting match; it means giving reasons. Opinions aren't bad, and everyone has them, but on their own they can't be checked. Once reasons are attached, other people can examine them: are the reasons true, and do they actually support the claim?
**Example:** "Pineapple on pizza is gross" is an opinion. "Pineapple on pizza is a bad idea because the juice makes the base soggy" is an argument. Now someone can reply, "Not if you bake it longer," and you're actually debating instead of just disagreeing.
**Why it matters:** When someone just repeats an opinion louder, ask "why?" If no reason comes back, there's nothing there to be persuaded by.

### Philosophy | Premises and conclusion
**In short:** Premises are the reasons; the conclusion is what they're meant to prove.
**Explained:** Every argument has at least one premise and one conclusion. Signal words help: "because", "since" and "given that" usually introduce premises; "so", "therefore" and "which means" introduce the conclusion. In real life the conclusion often comes first, and some premises are left unsaid. These are called hidden premises.
**Example:** "Don't buy that car, it's a diesel." Conclusion: don't buy it. Stated premise: it's a diesel. Hidden premise: diesel cars are a bad buy (fuel prices, city bans). The hidden premise is the part actually worth questioning.
**Why it matters:** The conclusion tells you what someone wants you to believe. The hidden premises show you where the argument is weakest.

### Philosophy | Valid vs sound
**In short:** Valid means the conclusion follows from the premises. Sound means it's valid *and* the premises are true.
**Explained:** Validity is about the shape of an argument, not the facts. Ask: if the premises were true, would the conclusion have to be true? If yes, it's valid, even if the premises are nonsense. Soundness adds the reality check. Only a sound argument guarantees a true conclusion.
**Example:** "All fish can fly. A salmon is a fish. So salmon can fly." Valid, but the first premise is false, so it's unsound. The flip side: "Some politicians lie. Bob is a politician. So Bob lies." The premises may well be true, but the conclusion doesn't follow. Invalid.
**Why it matters:** Arguments fail in two ways: bad logic or bad facts. Check both before you accept the conclusion.

### Economics | Scarcity
**In short:** Resources are limited but wants aren't, so choices have to be made.
**Explained:** Scarcity doesn't mean something is rare. It means there isn't enough for everyone to have as much as they want for free. Time, money, land, workers and attention are all scarce. So every person, business and government has to decide what to do, what to give up, and who gets what. Economics is basically the study of those choices.
**Example:** A hospital has a fixed budget and a limited number of doctors. Opening a new cancer ward might mean longer waits in the emergency room. Neither option is "free", even if the patient never sees a bill.
**Why it matters:** When a politician promises something with no downside, scarcity is the question to ask: what gets less so this can get more?

### Economics | Opportunity cost
**In short:** The real cost of something is the best alternative you gave up to get it.
**Explained:** Costs aren't only money. Time, effort and missed chances count too. Economists look at the *next best* option you didn't take, because that's what the choice really cost you. Two people can pay the same price for something and face very different opportunity costs.
**Example:** University costs tuition, but also the wages you could have earned working for those 3–4 years. For most people it's still worth it. For a talented young footballer, or a teenager whose business is taking off, the hidden cost can be huge, which is why some sensibly skip it.
**Why it matters:** Price tags hide the biggest costs. Asking "what am I giving up?" makes decisions, personal and political, much clearer.

### Politics | Power
**In short:** Power is the ability to get others to do what they otherwise wouldn't.
**Explained:** Power comes in several forms. Coercion uses force or threats. Reward uses money, jobs or favours. Authority works when people accept that someone has the *right* to give orders. The political theorist Steven Lukes added a quieter kind: shaping what people want, or think is even possible, so they never object in the first place.
**Example:** A boss can order you to stay late (authority), pay you overtime (reward), threaten your job (coercion), or build a culture where staying late is "just what committed people do" (shaping beliefs). The last one doesn't need an order at all.
**Why it matters:** Politics is largely about who holds power, how they got it, and whether they use it fairly. The quiet kind is the hardest to notice, and the most useful to spot.

### Politics | The state
**In short:** The state is the permanent machinery that makes and enforces rules over a territory.
**Explained:** The state includes the government, parliament, courts, police, army and civil service. It's different from *the government*, which is just the group running it at the moment. The sociologist Max Weber defined the state as the organisation that claims the only *legitimate* right to use force in a territory. Others can use force, but only the state's use is accepted as rightful.
**Example:** After an election the government changes, but the tax office, courts and army carry on. And if a private company locks you in a room, that's kidnapping; if the police detain you under the law, it's legitimate.
**Why it matters:** Arguments about "big" versus "small" government are really arguments about how much the state should do.

## Day II

### Philosophy | Deductive vs inductive reasoning
**In short:** Deduction goes from general rules to a certain conclusion; induction goes from observed cases to a likely one.
**Explained:** In a good deductive argument, if the premises are true the conclusion *must* be true. Induction goes beyond the evidence: it spots a pattern and predicts it will continue. That makes it how we learn about the world, but it's never 100% certain. Science mostly runs on induction, then keeps testing it.
**Example:** Deduction: "Every employee gets a payslip. Sam is an employee. So Sam gets a payslip." Induction: "Every swan I've ever seen is white, so all swans are white." Europeans believed exactly that for centuries, until black swans were found in Australia.
**Why it matters:** When someone says something is "proven", ask which kind. Deductive proof is airtight; inductive evidence is only strong or weak, and one new case can overturn it.

### Philosophy | Ad hominem
**In short:** Attacking the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.
**Explained:** Latin for "to the person". It's tempting because it's easy and feels like a win, but it says nothing about whether the claim is true. A bad person can make a good argument, and a good person a bad one. Pointing out a real conflict of interest is fair, but even that is a reason to check more carefully, not proof the argument is wrong.
**Example:** "Why listen to her views on tax? She failed maths at school." Her grades from 20 years ago say nothing about whether her tax plan works. Compare: "The study saying sugar is harmless was paid for by a soft-drink company." Worth knowing, and worth checking the study closely, but it doesn't make the study false by itself.
**Why it matters:** When a debate turns personal, it's often a sign someone has run out of arguments.

### Economics | Incentives
**In short:** Incentives are rewards and penalties that change what people choose to do.
**Explained:** People respond to costs and benefits: money, time, status, guilt or convenience. Change the incentives and behaviour usually changes, often in ways the rule-makers didn't expect. A badly designed incentive that rewards the very thing you wanted less of is called a "perverse incentive".
**Example:** In colonial India, officials in Delhi are said to have paid a bounty for dead cobras to cut their numbers. People started breeding cobras for the reward. When the scheme was scrapped, the breeders set their snakes free, leaving more cobras than before. The story may be partly legend, but the "cobra effect" happens all the time in real policies.
**Why it matters:** Before judging any rule, ask: "What will people actually do because of this?"

### Economics | Thinking at the margin
**In short:** Most real decisions are about a little more or a little less, not all or nothing.
**Explained:** Economists call the extra benefit of one more unit the *marginal benefit*, and the extra cost the *marginal cost*. A sensible rule: keep going while the extra benefit is bigger than the extra cost, and stop when it isn't. The value of each extra unit usually falls; the first slice of pizza is great, the fifth much less so.
**Example:** A café that closes at 9 pm wonders about staying open until 10. The question isn't "is the café profitable?" but "will that one extra hour bring in more than it costs in wages, electricity and tired staff?" The café can be profitable overall and still lose money on that last hour.
**Why it matters:** "Is one more worth it?" is usually a better question than "Is this good?"

### Economics | Sunk costs
**In short:** A sunk cost is money, time or effort already spent that you can't get back.
**Explained:** Because it's gone whatever you decide next, it shouldn't affect your next decision; only future costs and benefits should. But people hate "wasting" what they've put in, so they stick with bad choices. That's the sunk cost fallacy.
**Example:** You bought a concert ticket, but on the night you feel ill. The money is spent either way, so the only real question is: "Would I rather be at the concert or at home right now?" Britain and France kept funding the supersonic Concorde long after it was clearly a money-loser, partly because so much had already been spent. It's even called the "Concorde fallacy".
**Why it matters:** "We've come too far to stop now" is one of the most expensive sentences in business and politics.

### Politics | Authority
**In short:** Authority is power that people accept as rightful. They obey because they think they should, not just because they must.
**Explained:** A mugger has power over you; a police officer has authority. The difference isn't strength but acceptance. Authority is much cheaper and more stable than force: if most people follow the rules willingly, you don't need a soldier on every corner. It can come from an official role, from expertise, or from personal respect.
**Example:** A referee is usually smaller than the players, yet a player shown a red card walks off. That's authority. When players and fans stop accepting the referee's decisions, a match turns chaotic fast. The whistle only works because people believe in it.
**Why it matters:** Governments rarely fall because they run out of weapons. They fall when people stop accepting their authority.

### Politics | Legitimacy
**In short:** Legitimacy is the widely shared belief that someone has the right to rule.
**Explained:** Max Weber described three sources. *Traditional*: "it has always been this way", like hereditary kings. *Charismatic*: loyalty to an extraordinary leader. *Legal-rational*: power that comes from agreed rules, like elections and constitutions. Most modern states rely on the third, often with a bit of the other two mixed in.
**Example:** The British monarchy keeps traditional legitimacy even though it has little real power. Revolutionary leaders like Fidel Castro leaned heavily on charisma. A newly elected prime minister gets legitimacy from winning under agreed rules, which is why disputed elections are so dangerous: if people doubt the rules, they doubt the winner's right to rule.
**Why it matters:** Even dictators hold elections. They're chasing legitimacy, because ruling by force alone is expensive and fragile.

## Day III

### Philosophy | Straw man
**In short:** Twisting someone's argument into a weaker, exaggerated version, then knocking that down instead.
**Explained:** The name comes from a scarecrow: easy to beat because it can't fight back. It's popular because it makes you look like you've won without dealing with what the other person actually said. It often starts with "So what you're saying is…"
**Example:** A: "I think we should spend a bit less on the military and a bit more on schools." B: "So you want to leave the country defenceless?" A never said that. B is attacking an extreme position that's far easier to beat than the real one.
**Why it matters:** A good test in any debate: could the other side read your summary of their view and say "yes, that's what I mean"? If not, you might be arguing with a scarecrow.

### Philosophy | Appeal to authority
**In short:** Claiming something is true just because an important or famous person said so.
**Explained:** Trusting experts is often sensible; nobody can check everything themselves. It becomes a fallacy when the "authority" isn't an expert in that area, when real experts widely disagree, or when someone's status is used to shut down the evidence. The useful question isn't "who said it?" but "how would they know?"
**Example:** "A Nobel Prize-winning physicist says this diet cures cancer." Being brilliant at physics doesn't make someone an expert on nutrition. Compare: "The large majority of climate scientists agree the planet is warming." That's a reasonable appeal: relevant experts, broad agreement, and evidence you could check.
**Why it matters:** Advertising runs on this fallacy. A famous athlete selling a supplement, or an actor in a white coat, isn't evidence.

### Economics | Demand
**In short:** Demand is how much of something people are willing *and able* to buy at different prices.
**Explained:** The law of demand says that, other things being equal, when the price rises people buy less, and when it falls they buy more. "Able" matters: wanting a sports car isn't demand if you can't pay for it. Demand as a whole can also shift when tastes, incomes or the prices of related goods change.
**Example:** When a supermarket halves the price of strawberries and they sell out, that's a price effect. When a viral video makes a recipe famous and people buy more of an ingredient at the *same* price, that's demand itself shifting.
**Why it matters:** Sales can rise because something got cheaper or because people want it more. Knowing which one helps you read headlines about "booming" or "collapsing" markets.

### Economics | Supply
**In short:** Supply is how much of something producers are willing and able to sell at different prices.
**Explained:** Usually, the higher the price, the more producers want to sell, because it's more profitable and worth the extra effort. Supply as a whole shifts when production costs, technology, weather or the number of sellers change. Where supply meets demand, you get the market price.
**Example:** When coffee prices rise, farmers plant more coffee, and some switch over from other crops. But a frost in Brazil, the world's biggest coffee grower, can wipe out part of a harvest, cutting supply and pushing prices up worldwide, even though nobody's taste for coffee changed.
**Why it matters:** When prices jump, ask: did people want more, or could sellers offer less? The answer points to very different fixes.

### Politics | Sovereignty
**In short:** Sovereignty is supreme authority over a territory: no higher power inside it, and no outside power above it.
**Explained:** The modern idea is often traced to the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which ended decades of religious war in Europe with the principle that states shouldn't meddle in each other's internal affairs. Sovereignty has an internal side (the final say over laws at home) and an external side (being recognised as independent by other states).
**Example:** A country setting its own tax rates is exercising sovereignty. Joining the EU meant pooling some of it, for example by following shared trade rules. That's why the Brexit campaign's slogan was "Take back control."
**Why it matters:** Arguments about trade deals, international courts or foreign intervention are usually arguments about how much sovereignty a country should keep or share.

### Politics | Nation vs state
**In short:** A state is a political organisation with borders and a government. A nation is a group of people who feel they share an identity.
**Explained:** The two often overlap, giving us the "nation-state", but not always. A nation can exist without its own state, and one state can contain several nations. National identity can rest on language, history, religion, culture, or simply a shared story people believe in.
**Example:** The Kurds are a nation of roughly 30–40 million people spread across Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Syria, with no state of their own. The United Kingdom is one state containing several nations: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, which even play as separate football teams.
**Why it matters:** Many conflicts, from independence movements to civil wars, come from a mismatch between nations and state borders.

### Politics | Rule of law
**In short:** The rule of law means everyone, including those in power, must follow the same known laws, applied fairly.
**Explained:** It rests on a few ideas: laws are public and clear, they apply equally to everyone, courts are independent, and nobody can be punished except under the law. It's different from "rule *by* law", where rulers use laws as a weapon against others while staying above them.
**Example:** In South Korea, President Park Geun-hye was impeached in 2017, removed from office and later jailed for corruption. A sitting leader being held to the same law as everyone else is the rule of law working. In countries where courts never rule against the government, laws exist, but the rule of law doesn't.
**Why it matters:** Without the rule of law, rights on paper mean little, and business, investment and everyday trust all suffer.

## Day IV

### Philosophy | Tu quoque
**In short:** Dismissing someone's argument because they don't follow their own advice.
**Explained:** Latin for "you too". It points out hypocrisy, which may be true, but hypocrisy doesn't make the advice wrong. Whether a claim is true has nothing to do with whether the person saying it lives up to it.
**Example:** A parent who smokes tells their teenager not to start. The teenager replies, "You smoke, so why should I listen?" The parent is inconsistent, but the health risks of smoking are exactly the same either way.
**Why it matters:** "You do it too" is one of the most common replies in politics. It changes the subject from the idea to the person, and the original question never gets answered.

### Philosophy | False dilemma
**In short:** Presenting only two options when more actually exist.
**Explained:** Also called a false dichotomy or "black-and-white thinking". It forces a choice by quietly hiding the middle ground, the combinations and the alternatives. It works because two options feel simple and urgent.
**Example:** After the 9/11 attacks, US President George W. Bush said, "Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists." Plenty of countries were neither: they opposed terrorism but disagreed with parts of US policy.
**Why it matters:** Whenever you hear "either… or…", ask: are these really the only two choices? Usually there's a third option, and sometimes a fourth.

### Philosophy | Slippery slope
**In short:** Claiming that one small step will inevitably lead to a chain of extreme consequences.
**Explained:** It's a fallacy when the chain isn't backed by evidence at each step. Some chains are real, and some slopes really are slippery. The problem is assuming each step follows without showing why.
**Example:** "If we let students redo one test, soon they'll expect to redo every test, then nobody will study, and the whole school will collapse." Each step might be possible, but none is shown to be likely, and teachers can simply set limits.
**Why it matters:** Ask of each link in the chain: "Why would this lead to that?" If there's no answer, the scary ending is just imagination.

### Economics | Market equilibrium
**In short:** The price at which the amount people want to buy equals the amount sellers want to sell.
**Explained:** If the price is too high, goods go unsold and sellers cut prices. If it's too low, goods run out and sellers raise prices. These pushes tend to move the market towards a balance point, called equilibrium, where there's no pressure to change.
**Example:** A fruit seller at a night market starts at a high price for mangoes. By 9 pm, with half the crate left, she drops the price until they sell. The next week she starts a little lower. She's finding the equilibrium by trial and error.
**Why it matters:** Prices aren't just set by greedy sellers or kind ones. They're constantly pushed around by both sides of the market.

### Economics | Price signals
**In short:** Prices carry information that tells people what's scarce and what's plentiful, without anyone needing the full story.
**Explained:** The economist Friedrich Hayek argued in 1945 that a price packs huge amounts of scattered knowledge into one number. When something gets scarcer, its price rises, and people use less of it and look for substitutes, even if they have no idea why.
**Example:** Hayek's own example was tin. If a new use for tin appears or a mine closes, the price of tin rises. Millions of users cut back or switch materials, though almost none of them know what happened. The price did the coordinating.
**Why it matters:** When governments freeze prices, they also switch off this signal, which is why shortages often follow.

### Politics | Separation of powers
**In short:** Splitting government into separate branches so no single person or group holds all power.
**Explained:** The usual split is three branches: the legislature makes laws, the executive carries them out, and the judiciary interprets them. The French thinker Montesquieu argued for this in *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), and it strongly influenced the US Constitution.
**Example:** In the United States, Congress passes laws, the President enforces them, and the courts decide what they mean. In the UK, the lines are blurrier: the Prime Minister and ministers sit in Parliament, so the executive and legislature overlap.
**Why it matters:** Concentrated power is easy to abuse. Splitting it means one branch can't simply do whatever it wants.

### Politics | Checks and balances
**In short:** Ways each branch of government can limit the others.
**Explained:** Separation of powers divides power; checks and balances let the parts push back on each other. Each branch gets some tools to block or correct the others, so any big decision needs more than one branch to agree.
**Example:** In the US, the President can veto a bill, but Congress can override the veto with a two-thirds vote in both houses. The Senate must approve the President's choices for the Supreme Court. And the Court can strike down laws that break the Constitution.
**Why it matters:** Checks slow government down, which can be frustrating. But that friction is deliberate: it makes abuses of power harder.

## Day V

### Philosophy | Circular reasoning
**In short:** An argument that assumes the very thing it's trying to prove.
**Explained:** Also called "begging the question" (in its original meaning). The conclusion is hidden inside the premises, so the argument goes round in a circle and proves nothing. It can sound convincing because it's perfectly consistent with itself.
**Example:** "He's trustworthy." "How do you know?" "Because he told me he's honest." If he isn't trustworthy, his word is worthless, so it can't be the evidence. Or: "This brand is the best because it's better than all the others."
**Why it matters:** When an argument feels airtight but somehow tells you nothing new, check whether it's just going round in a circle.

### Philosophy | Red herring
**In short:** Bringing in an irrelevant topic to distract from the real question.
**Explained:** The name is often traced to the idea of dragging strongly smelling smoked fish across a trail to throw hunting dogs off the scent. The new topic may be interesting, even true, but it doesn't answer what was asked.
**Example:** A reporter asks a minister why hospital waiting times have grown. The minister replies, "What people really care about is that we've cut crime and created jobs." Those may be good things, but the question about waiting times has quietly disappeared.
**Why it matters:** Keep track of the original question. If the answer drifts somewhere else, that's often a sign there's no good answer to give.

### Economics | Shortages and surpluses
**In short:** A shortage is when people want more than is for sale at the current price; a surplus is when more is for sale than people want.
**Explained:** Both happen when the price is stuck away from equilibrium. A price below equilibrium creates a shortage (queues, empty shelves, resellers). A price above it creates a surplus (unsold stock). Without interference, prices usually adjust to clear them.
**Example:** When the PlayStation 5 launched in 2020, stores kept the official price while demand was huge and supply limited, so consoles sold out instantly and resold online for much more. In the 1970s–80s, the EU guaranteed farmers high prices and ended up with famous "butter mountains".
**Why it matters:** Queues and piles of unsold goods are both clues that a price isn't doing its job.

### Economics | Price ceilings
**In short:** A legal maximum price, set below what the market would charge.
**Explained:** Ceilings aim to keep essentials affordable. But when the price is held below equilibrium, more people want the good and sellers offer less of it, so shortages follow. Quality can also fall, and people spend time queuing or turn to black markets.
**Example:** Rent control is the classic case. In Stockholm, controlled rents are cheap, but the waiting list for a central apartment is many years long. The Swedish economist Assar Lindbeck famously joked that, apart from bombing, rent control is the best way to destroy a city.
**Why it matters:** A capped price helps the people who get the good, but it can hurt those who can't get it at all. Ask who ends up in the queue.

### Economics | Price floors
**In short:** A legal minimum price, set above what the market would charge.
**Explained:** Floors aim to protect sellers' incomes, such as farmers or workers. If the floor is set well above equilibrium, sellers offer more than buyers want, creating a surplus. For labour, that surplus would be unemployment.
**Example:** The minimum wage is a price floor on labour. Economists argue about its effects: a famous 1994 study by David Card and Alan Krueger found that a minimum wage rise in New Jersey didn't reduce fast-food jobs, while other studies find job losses, especially when the minimum is set high.
**Why it matters:** Whether a floor helps or hurts depends on how high it's set. The real debate is usually about the size, not the idea.

### Politics | Constitution
**In short:** The basic rules of a country: how government is organised, what powers it has, and what rights people hold.
**Explained:** A constitution is the "rules about the rules". It usually sits above ordinary laws and is harder to change. Most countries have a single written document, but not all: the UK's constitution is spread across laws, court rulings and long-standing conventions.
**Example:** The US Constitution, written in 1787, is often called the oldest written national constitution still in use. Changing it requires two-thirds of both houses of Congress and three-quarters of the states, which is why it has only 27 amendments.
**Why it matters:** When politicians argue that something is "unconstitutional", they're arguing that it breaks the rules everyone else must play by.

### Politics | Citizenship
**In short:** Legal membership of a state, with rights and duties attached.
**Explained:** Citizens usually get the right to vote, hold a passport and live in the country, and they may owe duties like paying taxes, jury service or military service. Countries grant it in two main ways: by birthplace (*jus soli*) or by parents' citizenship (*jus sanguinis*), plus naturalisation for newcomers.
**Example:** A baby born in the United States is automatically a US citizen, whatever their parents' nationality. Japan works mainly the other way: citizenship passes through parents, so being born in Japan isn't enough.
**Why it matters:** Debates about immigration are partly debates about citizenship: who belongs, and how someone becomes a member.

## Day VI

### Philosophy | Post hoc ergo propter hoc
**In short:** Assuming that because B happened after A, A must have caused B.
**Explained:** Latin for "after this, therefore because of this". Timing alone isn't proof of cause. Two events can follow each other by coincidence, or both can be caused by something else. It's the reasoning behind many superstitions.
**Example:** A rooster crows every morning just before sunrise, but it doesn't make the sun rise. A football fan wears the same socks and their team wins three times, so the socks become "lucky". A new mayor takes office and crime falls, but it had been falling for years before.
**Why it matters:** Politicians love taking credit for whatever happened after they arrived. Always ask what else might explain it.

### Philosophy | Correlation vs causation
**In short:** Two things moving together (correlation) doesn't mean one causes the other (causation).
**Explained:** A correlation can come from a third factor that drives both, from pure coincidence, or from causation running the other way. To show causation, you need more: an experiment, a clear mechanism, or ruling out the other explanations.
**Example:** Ice cream sales and drownings rise and fall together. Ice cream doesn't cause drowning: hot weather drives both, because more people buy ice cream and more people swim. Or: people who own more books tend to be healthier, but books probably aren't the cause; income and education affect both.
**Why it matters:** Headlines like "coffee linked to longer life" usually report a correlation. "Linked to" is not "causes".

### Economics | Elasticity of demand
**In short:** How strongly the amount people buy responds to a change in price.
**Explained:** Demand is *elastic* when a small price change causes a big change in buying, usually for things with easy substitutes. It's *inelastic* when people keep buying roughly the same amount, usually for necessities or addictive goods with few alternatives.
**Example:** If one brand of cola raises its price by 20%, many people simply switch brands: elastic. If petrol rises by 20%, most drivers still need to get to work, so they cut back only a little in the short run: inelastic.
**Why it matters:** Governments tax goods with inelastic demand, like tobacco and fuel, because people keep buying them, so the tax brings in steady money.

### Economics | Substitutes and complements
**In short:** Substitutes are goods you use instead of each other; complements are goods you use together.
**Explained:** When the price of one good changes, it affects demand for related goods. If tea gets more expensive, some people switch to coffee, so demand for coffee rises: they're substitutes. If games consoles get cheaper, more people buy games too: they're complements.
**Example:** Printer companies often sell printers cheaply and make their money on ink, a complement you must keep buying. This is known as the "razor and blades" model, after razor makers who did the same with replacement blades.
**Why it matters:** A price change never stays in one market. It ripples into the prices and sales of everything connected to it.

### Politics | Rights
**In short:** Entitlements that protect what a person may do or must be given.
**Explained:** Some thinkers, like John Locke, argued for *natural rights* that everyone has simply by being human, such as life, liberty and property. *Legal rights* are those a particular law grants. Another split: *negative rights* protect you from interference (free speech), *positive rights* entitle you to something (education).
**Example:** After the Second World War, the United Nations adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). It lists both kinds, from freedom from torture to the right to education, but it isn't directly enforceable like a national law.
**Why it matters:** Many political debates are clashes of rights: my right to free speech against your right to privacy, or property rights against the right to housing.

### Politics | Civil liberties
**In short:** Freedoms that protect individuals from government overreach.
**Explained:** They include freedom of speech, religion and assembly, the right to a fair trial, and protection from arbitrary arrest or unreasonable searches. Civil liberties limit what the state may do to you, and they're usually written into a constitution or bill of rights.
**Example:** *Habeas corpus*, strengthened in English law by the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679, lets a detained person demand to be brought before a court so a judge can decide whether the detention is lawful. Without it, a government could simply lock up its critics.
**Why it matters:** Civil liberties are often tested in emergencies, like wars and terror attacks, when governments ask for more power "just for now".

### Politics | Freedom of expression
**In short:** The right to express opinions and share information without government punishment.
**Explained:** It covers speech, writing, art and protest. John Stuart Mill argued in *On Liberty* (1859) that even wrong opinions should be heard, because challenging them keeps the truth alive. Almost every country sets some limits, such as fraud, threats or incitement to violence. The argument is about where to draw the line.
**Example:** The famous line about "falsely shouting fire in a theatre" comes from a 1919 US Supreme Court case that upheld jailing a man for leaflets against the military draft. In 1969, the Court replaced that test with a much narrower one: speech can only be punished if it's meant to incite imminent lawless action.
**Why it matters:** Who decides what counts as "dangerous" speech is one of the oldest fights in politics.

## Day VII

### Philosophy | Hasty generalisation
**In short:** Drawing a big conclusion from too few examples.
**Explained:** A small or unusual sample may not represent the whole group. The fewer and more memorable the examples, the more tempting the leap, and the less it can be trusted. Good generalisations need enough cases, chosen fairly.
**Example:** You visit a city for a weekend, meet two rude taxi drivers, and decide "people there are rude." Two drivers out of millions of residents tell you almost nothing. Or: a friend's cousin smoked until 95, "so smoking can't be that bad."
**Why it matters:** Many stereotypes start as hasty generalisations. Asking "how many cases is that based on?" stops a lot of bad conclusions.

### Philosophy | Cherry-picking
**In short:** Choosing only the evidence that supports your view and ignoring the rest.
**Explained:** Each piece of cherry-picked evidence may be completely true. The trick is in what's left out. By showing only the favourable parts, you can make almost anything look convincing, which makes it harder to spot than an outright lie.
**Example:** An investment fund advertises its three best years and quietly skips the bad ones. For years, some climate sceptics measured warming "since 1998", an unusually hot El Niño year, to make it look as if warming had stopped; picking almost any other start year showed it hadn't.
**Why it matters:** Ask: what does the *whole* picture look like? Where does the chart start, and what's missing?

### Philosophy | Survivorship bias
**In short:** Drawing conclusions only from the things that "survived", while missing the ones that didn't.
**Explained:** The failures are often invisible: they went out of business, stopped posting, or never came back. Looking only at the survivors makes success look easier, or makes the wrong factors look important.
**Example:** The classic story: in the Second World War, the statistician Abraham Wald studied bullet holes on bombers that returned from missions. Others wanted to add armour where the holes were. Wald pointed out the opposite: planes hit in other places, like the engines, hadn't made it back to be counted.
**Why it matters:** "Dropouts who became billionaires" advice ignores the far larger number of dropouts who didn't. Always ask about the ones you can't see.

### Economics | Production possibility frontier
**In short:** A graph showing the most a country (or person) can produce of two things with the resources it has.
**Explained:** Every point on the curve uses all resources fully. To get more of one thing, you must give up some of the other, which is opportunity cost made visible. Points inside the curve mean waste or unemployment; points outside are impossible, for now.
**Example:** Economists' classic example is "guns versus butter": a country can make more weapons or more food, but not unlimited amounts of both. During the Second World War, many factories switched from cars to tanks and planes, moving along the curve.
**Why it matters:** Growth, from better technology, skills or more workers, pushes the whole curve outward. That's the only way to get more of everything.

### Economics | Absolute advantage
**In short:** Being able to produce more of something than someone else, using the same resources.
**Explained:** If one country can make more cars per worker than another, it has an absolute advantage in cars. It sounds as if this should decide who makes what. But it doesn't, and understanding why is the key to comparative advantage, coming up next.
**Example:** A top lawyer can also type faster than her assistant, so she has an absolute advantage in both law and typing. Should she do her own typing? Probably not: every hour she spends typing is an hour of expensive legal work she isn't doing.
**Why it matters:** Being "better at everything" doesn't mean you should do everything. Time spent on one task is always time taken from another.

### Politics | Democracy
**In short:** A system where power ultimately comes from the people, usually through voting.
**Explained:** In a *direct* democracy, citizens vote on decisions themselves. In a *representative* democracy, they elect people to decide for them. Most modern democracies are representative, with some direct elements. Real democracy also needs free and fair elections, free speech and the rule of law.
**Example:** In ancient Athens, citizens voted directly in the Assembly, but women, enslaved people and foreigners were excluded, so most residents had no vote. Switzerland today still holds several national referendums a year, letting voters decide specific laws directly.
**Why it matters:** Many regimes call themselves democracies. Asking "can voters actually remove the people in power?" is a good test.

### Politics | Representation
**In short:** Elected people acting on behalf of the voters who chose them.
**Explained:** There's an old debate about how representatives should act. As *delegates*, they do what their voters want. As *trustees*, they use their own judgement, even against voters' wishes. The Irish-born politician Edmund Burke defended the trustee view in a famous speech to his voters in Bristol in 1774.
**Example:** A representative whose voters strongly oppose a new tax, but who believes it's necessary, faces exactly this choice. Burke told his voters that a representative owes them his judgement, and betrays them if he sacrifices it to their opinion. Six years later, he gave up his Bristol seat when it was clear he would lose.
**Why it matters:** When you're annoyed that a politician "ignored the voters", you're taking the delegate side of a 250-year-old argument.

## Day VIII

### Philosophy | Moving the goalposts
**In short:** Raising the standard of proof after the original standard has been met.
**Explained:** Someone asks for evidence, you provide it, and they suddenly demand something bigger or different. Because the target keeps moving, no evidence can ever be enough, which means they were never really open to changing their mind.
**Example:** "Show me one study that proves it." You share a study. "One study isn't enough, show me ten." You show ten. "Those were all funded by universities, so they're biased." Each new demand appears only after the last one is met.
**Why it matters:** Before a debate, it helps to agree what would count as convincing. If someone won't say, the goalposts are probably on wheels.

### Philosophy | No true Scotsman
**In short:** Protecting a generalisation from counterexamples by redefining the group to exclude them.
**Explained:** Named by the philosopher Antony Flew. Someone says "No Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge." Told that their uncle Angus does, they reply, "Well, no *true* Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge." The claim can no longer be proven wrong, because any exception is ruled out by definition.
**Example:** "No real fan would ever criticise the club's manager." "I've supported them for 30 years and I think the manager is terrible." "Then you're not a real fan." The definition of "real fan" was quietly changed to keep the claim safe.
**Why it matters:** Watch for "real", "true" and "proper" doing heavy lifting in an argument. They often hide a moved definition.

### Economics | Comparative advantage
**In short:** You should specialise in what you give up the least to produce, even if someone else is better at everything.
**Explained:** The economist David Ricardo explained it in 1817 with England and Portugal. Even if Portugal made both wine and cloth more efficiently, both countries gained if Portugal focused on wine and England on cloth, then traded. What matters is the *opportunity cost* of each task, not raw ability.
**Example:** Back to the lawyer who types faster than her assistant. Her hour of legal work is worth far more than an hour of typing, while the assistant gives up much less by typing. So she should do law, he should type, and both are better off.
**Why it matters:** It's the main reason economists support trade: countries, and people, gain by specialising, even when one side is "better" at everything.

### Economics | Gains from trade
**In short:** When people trade voluntarily, both sides usually end up better off.
**Explained:** In a free exchange, each side values what they get more than what they give up, or they wouldn't agree. So trade isn't one side winning and the other losing: value is created. That's true between neighbours, companies and countries.
**Example:** You pay a café £3 for a coffee. You'd rather have the coffee than the £3; the café would rather have the £3 than the coffee. Both walk away happier. Between countries, trade lets each specialise and buy the rest more cheaply than making it at home.
**Why it matters:** Trade also has losers, such as workers in industries that face new competition. The debate is usually about how to share the gains, not whether they exist.

### Economics | Division of labour
**In short:** Splitting work into small specialised tasks makes a group far more productive.
**Explained:** Specialists get faster with practice, don't waste time switching tasks, and can use tools built for one job. Adam Smith made this the opening theme of *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). He also warned that endlessly repeating one simple task could leave workers bored and dulled.
**Example:** Smith described a pin factory. One untrained worker alone might make one pin a day, twenty at most. But ten workers, each doing a few of the roughly eighteen steps, could make about 48,000 pins a day together, around 4,800 each.
**Why it matters:** Nearly everything you own was made by thousands of specialists who never met. That's the division of labour, stretched across the world.

### Politics | Majority rule
**In short:** Decisions are made by whatever option gets more than half the votes.
**Explained:** It's simple and fair in one sense: everyone's vote counts equally. But for important decisions, many systems demand more than a simple majority, called a *supermajority*, to make sure big changes have broad support and can't be pushed through by a narrow win.
**Example:** A club might elect its chair by simple majority. But amending the US Constitution needs two-thirds of both houses of Congress and then three-quarters of the states. Some countries require a supermajority to change their constitution, too.
**Why it matters:** Deciding which questions need 50%+1 and which need more is itself a big political choice.

### Politics | Tyranny of the majority
**In short:** The danger that a majority uses democracy to oppress a minority.
**Explained:** The phrase is associated with Alexis de Tocqueville's *Democracy in America* (1835) and John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* (1859). Winning a vote doesn't make a decision just. That's why many democracies put some rights beyond the reach of ordinary majorities.
**Example:** Bills of rights, independent courts and constitutional protections exist largely for this reason. In the US, courts struck down racial segregation laws in the 1950s and '60s that had been passed by elected state governments.
**Why it matters:** Democracy isn't only about what most people want. It's also about what no majority should be allowed to do.

## Day IX

### Philosophy | Appeal to nature
**In short:** Assuming something is good because it's "natural", or bad because it's "unnatural".
**Explained:** "Natural" sounds wholesome, but nature includes poisons, diseases and earthquakes. Whether something is natural tells you nothing about whether it's safe, healthy or right. The same goes for "chemical", which describes everything, including water.
**Example:** Arsenic and deadly nightshade are completely natural. Vaccines, glasses and heart surgery aren't, but they save lives every day. "All-natural" on a food label is a marketing choice, not evidence that it's better for you.
**Why it matters:** When "natural" is doing the persuading, ask what the actual evidence says about safety or benefit.

### Philosophy | Bandwagon fallacy
**In short:** Believing something is true or good because lots of people believe it.
**Explained:** Also called the appeal to popularity. Popularity is a clue about what's fashionable, not about what's true. Following the crowd feels safe, and advertisers and campaigns know it, so they tell you what "everyone" is doing.
**Example:** For most of history, most people believed the Sun went around the Earth. Being the majority view didn't make it true. Today: "Millions of people use this supplement, so it must work." Millions of people can be wrong at the same time.
**Why it matters:** Popularity is worth noticing, and it can point you to something worth checking. But it's never a substitute for the evidence.

### Economics | Diminishing returns
**In short:** Adding more of one input, while others stay fixed, eventually adds less and less extra output.
**Explained:** At first, extra workers or resources help a lot. But if something else is fixed, like space, machines or land, each additional unit contributes less than the one before, and eventually people get in each other's way.
**Example:** A small café with one coffee machine hires a second barista: service speeds up a lot. A third helps a bit. By the sixth, they're queuing for the same machine and bumping into each other. Early economists like Thomas Malthus and David Ricardo applied the same idea to farming land.
**Why it matters:** "More" isn't always better. The useful question is: what does the *next* unit add?

### Economics | Economies of scale
**In short:** Producing more can make each unit cheaper to make.
**Explained:** Big producers spread fixed costs, like factories and design, over more units, buy materials in bulk, and use specialised machines. So the average cost per unit falls as output rises. Past a certain size, costs can rise again as organisations get hard to manage.
**Example:** Henry Ford's moving assembly line made the Model T far cheaper to build. Its price fell from about $850 in 1908 to around $260 by 1925, putting cars within reach of ordinary families.
**Why it matters:** Economies of scale help explain why some industries end up dominated by a few giant firms, and why small shops often can't match supermarket prices.

### Politics | Political parties
**In short:** Organised groups that compete in elections to win power and put their ideas into practice.
**Explained:** Parties bundle candidates, policies and supporters together, which helps voters make sense of choices. How many parties a country has often depends on its voting system. The political scientist Maurice Duverger observed that "first past the post" voting tends to produce two big parties.
**Example:** The US and UK, both using first past the post, are dominated by two main parties, though the UK has strong smaller ones too. Countries with proportional representation, like the Netherlands, often have many parties and rule through coalitions.
**Why it matters:** Parties shape the choices on your ballot long before you vote, by deciding who gets to stand.

### Politics | Interest groups
**In short:** Organised groups that try to influence government decisions without running for office themselves.
**Explained:** They include businesses, trade unions, professional bodies, charities and campaign groups. They lobby politicians, fund campaigns and shape public debate. The economist Mancur Olson argued in 1965 that small groups with a lot at stake organise more easily than large groups with a little each at stake.
**Example:** US tariffs and quotas on imported sugar raise sugar prices for millions of consumers by a small amount each, while a relatively small number of sugar producers gain a lot. The producers have strong reasons to lobby; consumers barely notice.
**Why it matters:** When a policy seems to help a few at the expense of many, Olson's logic is often the explanation.

### Politics | Civil society
**In short:** The groups and organisations people form outside government and business.
**Explained:** Clubs, charities, religious groups, unions, volunteer associations and neighbourhood groups make up civil society. Tocqueville admired how often Americans formed associations, and saw them as training grounds for democracy, where people learn to cooperate and organise.
**Example:** In *Bowling Alone* (2000), the political scientist Robert Putnam argued that Americans were joining fewer clubs and groups, even bowling alone instead of in leagues, and that this weakened trust and community life.
**Why it matters:** A strong civil society gives people a voice and a network beyond the state, which is why authoritarian governments often try to control or shut it down.

## Day X

### Philosophy | Confirmation bias
**In short:** The tendency to notice, search for and believe information that supports what you already think.
**Explained:** It works quietly: we remember the hits and forget the misses, read friendly sources, and hold unwelcome evidence to a higher standard. In a classic 1960 experiment, the psychologist Peter Wason found that people testing a number rule mostly tried examples that would confirm their guess, rarely ones that could disprove it.
**Example:** Someone who believes a full moon makes people act strangely remembers every chaotic full-moon night and forgets the calm ones. Online, algorithms make it worse by showing you more of what you already click on.
**Why it matters:** The best test of a belief is to look for evidence *against* it. If you can't imagine what would change your mind, that's a warning sign.

### Philosophy | Motivated reasoning
**In short:** Reasoning towards the conclusion you want, rather than following the evidence where it leads.
**Explained:** It's confirmation bias with a goal. When a belief is tied to our identity, team or self-image, we become skilled lawyers for our side, finding reasons to accept friendly facts and dismiss unfriendly ones. Being clever doesn't protect you; it can make you better at defending what you already believe.
**Example:** In a classic 1954 study, "They Saw a Game", Princeton and Dartmouth students watched the same film of a rough football match between their colleges. Princeton students "saw" Dartmouth commit about twice as many fouls as Dartmouth students did.
**Why it matters:** When you badly want something to be true, that's exactly when to be most careful.

### Philosophy | Anchoring
**In short:** Relying too heavily on the first number you see when making an estimate or decision.
**Explained:** The first figure becomes an "anchor", and later judgements stay too close to it, even when it's obviously irrelevant. In a famous 1974 experiment, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman spun a wheel rigged to land on 10 or 65, then asked people what percentage of UN members were African countries. Those who saw 65 guessed much higher.
**Example:** A jacket labelled "was £200, now £99" feels like a bargain because £200 is the anchor, even if nobody ever paid £200. In salary talks, the first number mentioned often shapes the final deal.
**Why it matters:** Before you see someone else's number, decide your own estimate first.

### Economics | The invisible hand
**In short:** Adam Smith's idea that people pursuing their own interest in markets can end up benefiting others without meaning to.
**Explained:** Smith used the phrase only once in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). His point: a baker bakes to earn a living, not out of kindness, yet the town gets its bread. Competition channels self-interest into serving customers. Smith also warned about monopolies and businesses colluding against the public, so he wasn't saying markets are always right.
**Example:** In Smith's words: "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."
**Why it matters:** The idea is often stretched to mean "leave markets alone". Smith's actual view was more careful, and knowing that helps you spot when he's being misquoted.

### Economics | Unintended consequences
**In short:** Results of an action or policy that nobody planned or predicted.
**Explained:** The sociologist Robert Merton popularised the idea in 1936. They happen because people react to rules in ways designers don't expect, and because every system has knock-on effects. They can be good, bad, or the opposite of what was intended.
**Example:** The US banned alcohol from 1920 to 1933. Drinking didn't disappear; it moved underground, and the huge profits from illegal alcohol helped organised crime grow, including gangsters like Al Capone. The ban was eventually repealed.
**Why it matters:** For any big new rule, ask: "And then what?" The second and third effects often matter more than the first.

### Politics | Public opinion
**In short:** What the public, taken as a whole, thinks about political issues and leaders.
**Explained:** Politicians watch it closely because it shapes elections and what policies they think they can get away with. It's usually measured by opinion polls, which survey a sample of people. A poll is only as good as its sample: who gets asked matters more than how many.
**Example:** In 1936, the *Literary Digest* magazine polled over two million people and predicted a big defeat for President Franklin Roosevelt. He won in a landslide. Its sample leaned towards wealthier people, while George Gallup correctly called the result with a much smaller but better-chosen sample.
**Why it matters:** Treat a single poll with care. Look at who was asked, how, and whether other polls agree.

### Politics | Political participation
**In short:** The ways ordinary people take part in politics.
**Explained:** Voting is the most common, but participation also includes joining a party, campaigning, signing petitions, protesting, contacting a representative, volunteering, or boycotting products. How many people take part, and who, affects whose interests politicians pay attention to.
**Example:** Australia has had compulsory voting in federal elections since 1924, and turnout is usually around 90%. In many countries without it, turnout is far lower, especially among young people, which gives older voters more weight.
**Why it matters:** Politicians focus on people who vote. Groups that don't take part tend to get overlooked.

## Day XI

### Philosophy | Availability heuristic
**In short:** Judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind.
**Explained:** A mental shortcut, described by Tversky and Kahneman. Vivid, recent or heavily reported events feel more common than they are, while dull everyday risks feel smaller. News coverage makes this worse, because rare dramatic events get the most attention.
**Example:** After the 9/11 attacks, many Americans avoided flying and drove instead. Driving is far riskier per mile, and the psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer estimated that around 1,600 extra people died on US roads in the following year as a result.
**Why it matters:** When something feels scary, check the actual numbers. The risks that make headlines are rarely the ones most likely to affect you.

### Philosophy | Dunning–Kruger effect
**In short:** People with little skill in an area tend to overestimate their ability, partly because they lack the knowledge to see their mistakes.
**Explained:** Named after psychologists David Dunning and Justin Kruger, from a 1999 study where the lowest scorers on tests of logic and grammar rated themselves well above average. Some researchers argue part of the effect is a statistical quirk, so it's less dramatic than internet memes suggest.
**Example:** After a few YouTube videos, a beginner feels ready to argue with an expert, while the expert, aware of everything that can go wrong, sounds much more cautious. The more you learn about a subject, the more you realise how much you don't know.
**Why it matters:** Confidence isn't the same as competence, in yourself or in others.

### Economics | Positive vs normative economics
**In short:** Positive statements describe what *is* and can be tested; normative statements say what *should be* and depend on values.
**Explained:** "Raising the tax on cigarettes will reduce smoking" is positive: evidence can check it. "The government should raise the tax on cigarettes" is normative: it depends on how you weigh health, freedom and fairness. Most real arguments mix the two.
**Example:** Two economists may fully agree that a higher minimum wage would raise some workers' pay and cost some others their jobs (positive), yet disagree about whether it's worth it (normative), because they value those outcomes differently.
**Why it matters:** Separating the facts from the values makes arguments clearer: you'll know whether you disagree about what's true or about what matters.

### Economics | Economic models
**In short:** Simplified versions of the economy, used to understand one part of it.
**Explained:** Like a map, a model leaves out most details on purpose to show what matters for one question. A map that included every tree would be useless. The statistician George Box put it well: "All models are wrong, but some are useful." The skill lies in knowing what a model leaves out.
**Example:** The supply and demand model ignores brand loyalty, advertising and moods, yet it still predicts that a frost destroying coffee crops will push coffee prices up. It fails, though, when the details it ignores turn out to matter, as many economists found in the 2008 financial crisis.
**Why it matters:** When someone says "economists predict…", ask what the model assumes, and whether those assumptions fit reality.

### Economics | Ceteris paribus
**In short:** Latin for "other things being equal": looking at the effect of one change while holding everything else constant.
**Explained:** Economists use it to isolate cause and effect. "When the price rises, people buy less, *ceteris paribus*." In the real world, other things rarely stay equal, so the phrase is a reminder of what an economic claim does and doesn't cover.
**Example:** Umbrella prices rise, yet umbrella sales go up too. Does that disprove the law of demand? No: it started raining. The weather changed, so other things weren't equal.
**Why it matters:** Many "gotchas" against economic ideas forget that something else changed at the same time.

### Politics | Consent of the governed
**In short:** The idea that a government is only legitimate if the people it rules agree to be ruled.
**Explained:** John Locke argued that people consent to government, sometimes openly, sometimes "tacitly", for example simply by living in a country and using its protection. Critics ask whether anyone really consents to a state they were born into and can't easily leave.
**Example:** The US Declaration of Independence (1776) states that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed". Elections are the main way modern democracies try to show that consent in practice.
**Why it matters:** When a government loses the consent of its people, as in mass protests or boycotted elections, its legitimacy is in trouble, even if it still controls the army.

### Politics | The social contract
**In short:** The idea that people agree, in effect, to give up some freedoms to a government in exchange for order and protection.
**Explained:** Thomas Hobbes, in *Leviathan* (1651), imagined life without government as a war of all against all, "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". John Locke thought people keep natural rights the government must protect. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in *The Social Contract* (1762), put the people's collective will at the centre.
**Example:** You accept laws against theft and pay taxes; in return, the state protects your property and safety. Nobody signed this contract, which is why philosophers describe it as a thought experiment rather than a real agreement.
**Why it matters:** Arguments about how much the state may demand of you, like taxes or lockdowns, often come back to what the "contract" really includes.

## Day XII

### Philosophy | Burden of proof
**In short:** The person making a claim is the one who has to support it.
**Explained:** You don't have to disprove every claim someone makes; they have to give reasons for it. The bigger the claim, the stronger the evidence needed. Courts follow a similar rule: the prosecution must prove guilt, rather than the accused proving innocence.
**Example:** The philosopher Bertrand Russell imagined someone claiming a tiny china teapot orbits the Sun between Earth and Mars, too small to see with telescopes. You can't disprove it, but that doesn't mean you should believe it. The burden is on the person who claims it's there.
**Why it matters:** "You can't prove it's not true" is not evidence. Hand the burden back to whoever made the claim.

### Philosophy | Occam's razor
**In short:** When two explanations fit the facts equally well, prefer the one that needs fewer assumptions.
**Explained:** Named after William of Ockham, a 14th-century English friar. It's a rule of thumb, not a law: the simpler explanation isn't always true, but it's usually the better place to start. Each extra assumption is another thing that could be wrong.
**Example:** You hear scratching in the attic at night. It could be a mouse, or a ghost that only appears when nobody's looking. Both "fit" the noise, but the mouse needs no new assumptions about how the world works.
**Why it matters:** Conspiracy theories usually need a huge number of extra assumptions. That alone doesn't disprove them, but it should make you much more sceptical.

### Economics | Rational choice
**In short:** The assumption that people make choices to get what they value most, given what they know and what they can afford.
**Explained:** In economics, "rational" doesn't mean selfish, clever or cold. It means choices are consistent with a person's own goals. Real people often fall short, with habits, biases and limited information, which later "behavioural economics" studies in detail.
**Example:** Someone who gives money to charity can be perfectly rational: they value helping others more than the money. But someone who pays for a gym membership and never goes, month after month, is probably not acting in line with their own goals.
**Why it matters:** Rational choice is a useful starting assumption, not a claim that people are perfect. Knowing where it breaks down is just as important.

### Economics | Cost-benefit analysis
**In short:** Adding up all the costs and benefits of a decision, usually in money terms, to see if it's worth doing.
**Explained:** Governments use it to judge roads, regulations and health programmes. The hard part is putting numbers on things like time saved, pollution or lives. Many governments even use a "value of a statistical life", often several million dollars, to decide which safety rules are worth their cost.
**Example:** A city is deciding whether to build a new bridge. Costs: construction, maintenance, noise. Benefits: shorter journeys, fewer accidents, new businesses. If benefits clearly outweigh costs, it's a strong case; if they're close, the choice depends on what's hard to measure.
**Why it matters:** It forces hidden costs and benefits into the open. But the result is only as good as the numbers and values fed into it.

### Politics | Accountability
**In short:** People in power having to explain their actions and face consequences for them.
**Explained:** Accountability works through several channels: elections (voters can remove leaders), courts (officials can be prosecuted), parliaments (ministers can be questioned), auditors (spending is checked) and a free press (wrongdoing gets exposed). Without accountability, power drifts towards abuse.
**Example:** In the Watergate scandal, reporting by *The Washington Post* and investigations by Congress revealed that President Richard Nixon had tried to cover up a break-in at his opponents' headquarters. Facing near-certain impeachment, he resigned in 1974.
**Why it matters:** The question to ask about any power is: who can hold it to account, and how?

### Politics | Transparency
**In short:** Making government information and decisions open to public view.
**Explained:** Transparency lets citizens, journalists and courts see what the government does, which makes accountability possible. Freedom of information laws give people the right to request official documents. Sweden passed one of the first, as part of its Freedom of the Press Act of 1766.
**Example:** Freedom of information requests by journalists have uncovered wasteful spending, safety failures and misleading statements by officials in many countries. Louis Brandeis, later a US Supreme Court justice, put the idea simply in 1913: "Sunlight is said to be the best of disinfectants."
**Why it matters:** Secrecy isn't always wrong, for example in national security. But the more a government hides, the harder it is to judge whether it deserves trust.

### Politics | Corruption
**In short:** The abuse of entrusted power for private gain.
**Explained:** That's the definition used by the anti-corruption group Transparency International. It ranges from *petty* corruption, like small bribes to police or clerks, to *grand* corruption by top officials. It wastes public money, rewards the wrong people and destroys trust in institutions.
**Example:** In the 1970s, Hong Kong's police force was notorious for corruption. In 1974, the government created the Independent Commission Against Corruption (ICAC), with strong powers and independence, and Hong Kong became known as one of the cleaner places in Asia.
**Why it matters:** Corruption isn't just about bad individuals. It thrives where accountability and transparency are weak, which is why institutions matter so much.

## Day XIII

### Philosophy | Necessary vs sufficient conditions
**In short:** A necessary condition must be present for something to happen; a sufficient condition guarantees it happens.
**Explained:** Oxygen is *necessary* for a fire, but not *sufficient*: you also need fuel and heat. Being a square is *sufficient* for being a rectangle, but not *necessary*, since other rectangles exist. Mixing the two up leads to many bad arguments.
**Example:** "A degree is necessary for this job" means no degree, no job, but having one doesn't guarantee you'll get it. A politician who says "economic growth is all we need" is treating growth as sufficient for a good society, which is a much bigger claim than saying it's necessary.
**Why it matters:** When someone says "you need X" or "X is enough", ask which one they really mean.

### Philosophy | Falsifiability
**In short:** A claim is scientific only if some possible evidence could prove it wrong.
**Explained:** The philosopher Karl Popper argued that science advances by making bold, testable predictions that risk failing. A theory that can explain away *any* result isn't strong; it's untestable. Popper criticised some theories of his time, such as parts of Freud's psychoanalysis, for exactly this.
**Example:** Einstein's theory of general relativity predicted that the Sun's gravity would bend starlight by a precise amount. In 1919, Arthur Eddington's expedition measured it during a solar eclipse. If the light hadn't bent as predicted, the theory would have been in trouble. It passed.
**Why it matters:** If a claim fits every possible outcome, like a horoscope, it isn't telling you anything.

### Philosophy | Base rates
**In short:** How common something is in the first place, which people often ignore when judging evidence.
**Explained:** Ignoring it is called *base rate neglect*. We focus on the specific, vivid information in front of us and forget the background odds, which can completely change what the evidence means.
**Example:** A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test catches every real case but wrongly flags 1% of healthy people. You test positive: what are the odds you're ill? Out of 1,000 people, 1 is ill and about 10 healthy people also test positive. So only about 1 in 11 positives is real, around 9%.
**Why it matters:** A "99% accurate" test, profile or warning sign can still be wrong most of the time if the thing it looks for is rare.

### Economics | Time value of money
**In short:** Money today is worth more than the same amount in the future.
**Explained:** Money you have now can be saved or invested to earn interest, prices tend to rise over time, and the future is uncertain. So to wait, people want to be paid extra. That extra is interest. Over years, interest earned on interest, called *compound interest*, grows surprisingly fast.
**Example:** At 7% a year, money roughly doubles in about 10 years. A handy shortcut is the "rule of 72": divide 72 by the interest rate to estimate how many years it takes to double. The same maths works against you with debt, like unpaid credit cards.
**Why it matters:** "Pay later" deals, loans, pensions and savings all rest on this idea. Time is part of the price.

### Economics | Risk and return
**In short:** To earn a higher expected return, you usually have to accept more risk.
**Explained:** Safe options, like a government-backed savings account, pay low but steady returns. Riskier ones, like company shares, can pay more on average, but with bigger swings and a real chance of losing money. *Diversification*, spreading money across many investments, reduces risk without giving up much return.
**Example:** Over long periods, stock markets have usually beaten savings accounts, but with painful drops along the way: in the 2008 financial crisis, many stock markets fell by around half before recovering. (This is how the idea works, not investment advice.)
**Why it matters:** When anything promises high returns with no risk, be suspicious. That combination is the signature of many scams.

### Politics | Bureaucracy
**In short:** The system of appointed officials who carry out the government's work, following set rules.
**Explained:** Max Weber saw bureaucracy as the most efficient way to run a large modern state: clear rules, a chain of command, written records, and officials hired for their skills rather than their connections. Its downside is "red tape": rigid rules, slow decisions and passing the blame.
**Example:** Imperial China ran one of history's earliest merit-based bureaucracies, using written civil service exams for over a thousand years, from the 600s until 1905. Many modern countries adopted merit-based civil services in the 19th century to replace hiring based on political favours.
**Why it matters:** Elections change the leaders, but bureaucrats run the state day to day. How good they are shapes almost every public service you use.

### Politics | Government vs governance
**In short:** Government is the institutions with official power; governance is the wider process of steering and managing, which involves many other players.
**Explained:** Governance includes businesses, charities, international organisations, experts and online communities that make and enforce rules alongside governments. As problems cross borders, like climate, pandemics and the internet, more of the steering happens outside any single government.
**Example:** No government runs the internet's address system. It's coordinated by ICANN, a non-profit organisation, along with technical standards bodies, companies and governments. That's governance without a single government in charge.
**Why it matters:** When you ask "who decides this?", the answer is often a mix of public and private players, not just the government.

## Day XIV

### Philosophy | Principle of charity
**In short:** Interpreting someone's argument in its most reasonable form before responding to it.
**Explained:** When a statement could mean several things, you assume the sensible version rather than the silliest one. It's not about agreeing; it's about making sure you're disagreeing with what the person actually means. It also makes conversations calmer and more productive.
**Example:** Someone says "the rich should pay more tax." The uncharitable reading is "take everything from anyone successful." The charitable reading is "people on high incomes should pay a somewhat higher rate." Arguing against the first version wins nothing.
**Why it matters:** It's the cure for the straw man, and people are far more willing to listen when they feel understood.

### Philosophy | Steelmanning
**In short:** Building the strongest possible version of the other side's argument, and then responding to that.
**Explained:** The opposite of a straw man. The philosopher Daniel Dennett suggested a rule: restate your opponent's position so clearly and fairly that they say "Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way." Only then start your criticism.
**Example:** Before arguing against a ban on cars in a city centre, you first state the best case for it: cleaner air, fewer accidents, livelier streets, and success in other cities. If your objections still hold up against that version, your argument is strong.
**Why it matters:** If you can only beat the weak version of a view, you haven't really beaten it. Steelmanning also shows you where you might be wrong.

### Economics | Moral hazard
**In short:** When people are protected from the costs of a risk, they tend to take more of it.
**Explained:** The term comes from insurance. Someone fully insured has less reason to be careful, because someone else pays if things go wrong. It happens whenever one side takes risks while another side carries the costs.
**Example:** People tend to drive a rental car harder than their own. On a bigger scale, when governments bailed out banks in the 2008 financial crisis, critics warned that big banks would learn they were "too big to fail" and keep taking huge risks, expecting to be rescued again.
**Why it matters:** Any safety net, from insurance to bailouts, changes behaviour. Good designs, like making people pay part of the cost, try to limit that.

### Economics | Principal–agent problem
**In short:** When someone acting on your behalf (the agent) has different interests from you (the principal).
**Explained:** You hire agents because they have skills or time you don't: managers, lawyers, estate agents, politicians. But they may put their own interests first, and you can't watch everything they do. Contracts, commissions and bonuses try to line up their interests with yours, with mixed success.
**Example:** In *Freakonomics*, Steven Levitt and Stephen Dubner reported that estate agents selling their *own* homes kept them on the market longer and sold for more. With a client's house, a slightly higher price barely changes the agent's commission, but a quick sale saves them time.
**Why it matters:** Whenever someone acts for you, ask how they get paid. It often explains what they recommend.

### Economics | Goodhart's law
**In short:** When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.
**Explained:** Named after the economist Charles Goodhart, who made the point about monetary policy in 1975; the popular wording came later from the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern. Once people are rewarded for hitting a number, they find ways to hit the number without achieving what it was meant to measure.
**Example:** If schools are judged only on exam results, some start "teaching to the test", drilling exam technique instead of deeper understanding. Scores rise, but learning may not. Or a call centre judged on call length finds staff rushing customers off the phone.
**Why it matters:** Targets in business and government can backfire. Watch what people do to hit the number, not just the number itself.

### Politics | Revolution
**In short:** A rapid, fundamental change in who holds power and how a society is governed, usually driven by mass movements.
**Explained:** Revolutions go further than a change of government: they overturn the whole system, often its economy and social order too. They can be violent or peaceful, and their results often differ from what the revolutionaries hoped.
**Example:** The French Revolution of 1789 overthrew the monarchy but was followed by the Terror and, later, Napoleon's rule. The Russian Revolution of 1917 led to the Soviet Union. Czechoslovakia's "Velvet Revolution" of 1989 ended communist rule through peaceful mass protests.
**Why it matters:** Revolutions show how fast legitimacy can collapse once enough people stop accepting a government's authority.

### Politics | Coup d'état
**In short:** A sudden, illegal seizure of power by a small group, usually the military.
**Explained:** French for "stroke of the state". Unlike a revolution, a coup is carried out by insiders, like generals or officials, rather than mass movements. It replaces the people at the top, often by force, and usually suspends normal politics.
**Example:** In 1973, Chile's military, led by General Augusto Pinochet, overthrew the elected President Salvador Allende and ruled for 17 years. Thailand has had more than a dozen successful coups since 1932.
**Why it matters:** Coups show that controlling the army and other forces can matter more than winning votes, which is why democracies keep the military under civilian control.

## Day XV

### Philosophy | Thought experiments
**In short:** Imagined scenarios used to test an idea, without doing a real experiment.
**Explained:** By asking "what would happen if…?", you can expose contradictions or hidden assumptions using reasoning alone. Philosophers and scientists both use them, especially where real experiments are impossible, unethical or too expensive.
**Example:** Galileo challenged the old belief that heavy objects fall faster. Imagine tying a heavy stone to a light one. The light one should slow the heavy one down, but together they're heavier and should fall faster. Both can't be true, so the old belief must be wrong. The trolley problem, posed by Philippa Foot in 1967, is a famous example in ethics.
**Why it matters:** A good thought experiment can overturn a belief in a few sentences.

### Philosophy | The Socratic method
**In short:** Exploring an idea through questions that test it, instead of simply stating answers.
**Explained:** Socrates, in 5th-century BC Athens, wrote nothing down; we know him mostly through his student Plato. He asked people to define ideas like justice or courage, then asked questions that exposed problems in their answers, until both sides saw how little they really knew. He was put on trial and executed in 399 BC.
**Example:** In Plato's dialogue *Laches*, two generals are asked what courage is. One says it's standing firm in battle; Socrates points out that a strategic retreat can also be courageous. Each new definition meets a new problem.
**Why it matters:** Asking good questions, of others and of yourself, is often more useful than having quick answers. That's the thread running through this whole Folio.

### Economics | Zero-sum vs positive-sum
**In short:** In a zero-sum situation, one side's gain is another's loss; in a positive-sum one, everyone can gain.
**Explained:** Poker is zero-sum: the money on the table just moves between players. Most voluntary trade is positive-sum: both sides gain, because value is created. Many economic mistakes come from treating positive-sum situations as zero-sum.
**Example:** The old *mercantilist* view saw trade as a contest in which a country got rich by exporting more and importing less, as if trade were zero-sum. Adam Smith and later economists argued that both trading countries can gain, because each gets goods more cheaply than making them itself.
**Why it matters:** If you think every deal has a loser, you'll see enemies everywhere. Asking "can both sides win here?" opens up better options.

### Economics | The seen and the unseen
**In short:** A good economist looks not only at the visible effects of a decision, but also at the hidden ones.
**Explained:** The French economist Frédéric Bastiat made this point in an 1850 essay. The obvious effects of a policy are easy to see; the costs, like what people would have done with the money instead, are invisible. It's opportunity cost, applied to whole economies.
**Example:** Bastiat's "broken window": a boy breaks a shop window, and people say it's good for the economy because the glazier gets work. That's the seen. The unseen: the shopkeeper would have spent that money on having his shoes mended or on a new book, so the shoemaker or bookseller loses the work. Society is one window poorer, not richer.
**Why it matters:** When you hear that disasters or wars "boost the economy", remember the broken window.

### Politics | Civil disobedience
**In short:** Deliberately and openly breaking a law you believe is unjust, without violence, and accepting the punishment.
**Explained:** The American writer Henry David Thoreau defended the idea in 1849, after refusing to pay a tax that supported slavery and the war with Mexico. The openness and acceptance of punishment are key: the aim is to show the law's injustice and appeal to the public's conscience.
**Example:** In 1930, Gandhi led the Salt March, openly making salt in defiance of Britain's tax on it. In 1955, Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat in Montgomery, Alabama, sparking a year-long boycott. Martin Luther King Jr. defended civil disobedience in his 1963 "Letter from Birmingham Jail".
**Why it matters:** It raises a hard question: when, if ever, is it right to break the law in a democracy?

### Politics | Political culture
**In short:** The shared attitudes, values and habits that shape how people in a country think about and take part in politics.
**Explained:** It includes how much people trust government, how they feel about authority, whether they expect to have a say, and how they handle disagreement. In *The Civic Culture* (1963), political scientists Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba compared five countries, including the US, UK and Mexico.
**Example:** In Nordic countries like Denmark, trust in government and in other people tends to be high, and people generally pay taxes willingly. Where trust is low, the same laws may be widely ignored or dodged. Copying another country's institutions doesn't automatically copy its political culture.
**Why it matters:** Two countries with similar constitutions can work very differently. Culture is often the reason.

### Politics | The political spectrum
**In short:** The common way of arranging political views from left to right.
**Explained:** The terms come from the French Revolution: in the National Assembly of 1789, supporters of the king sat on the right and supporters of change on the left. Today "left" usually means favouring more equality and state action, and "right" more tradition, markets and individual responsibility, but the meaning shifts between countries.
**Example:** A single line hides a lot. Someone can favour free markets but liberal social policies, or the reverse. That's why some models use two axes, one for economic views and one for social views, instead of one line.
**Why it matters:** "Left" and "right" are useful shorthand, but real views rarely fit neatly. It's the starting point for the Ideologies strand coming in a later Folio.
