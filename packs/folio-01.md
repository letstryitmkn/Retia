# Folio I
Philosophy: Thinking tools
Economics: Thinking like an economist
Politics: Core concepts

## Day I

### Philosophy | Argument vs opinion
**In short:** An opinion is a claim. An argument is a claim backed by reasons meant to support it.
**Explained:** In philosophy, "argument" doesn't mean a shouting match; it means giving reasons. Opinions aren't bad, and everyone has them, but on their own they can't be checked. Once reasons are attached, other people can examine them: are the reasons true, and do they actually support the claim?
**Example:** "Pineapple on pizza is gross" is an opinion. "Pineapple on pizza is a bad idea because the juice makes the base soggy" is an argument. Now someone can reply, "Not if you bake it longer," and you're actually debating instead of just disagreeing.
**Why it matters:** When someone just repeats an opinion louder, ask "why?" If no reason comes back, there's nothing there to be persuaded by.

### Philosophy | Premises and conclusion
**In short:** Premises are the reasons; the conclusion is what they're meant to prove.
**Explained:** Every argument has at least one premise and one conclusion. Signal words help: "because", "since" and "given that" usually introduce premises; "so", "therefore" and "which means" introduce the conclusion. In real life the conclusion often comes first, and some premises are left unsaid. These are called hidden premises.
**Example:** "Don't buy that car, it's a diesel." Conclusion: don't buy it. Stated premise: it's a diesel. Hidden premise: diesel cars are a bad buy (fuel prices, city bans). The hidden premise is the part actually worth questioning.
**Why it matters:** The conclusion tells you what someone wants you to believe. The hidden premises show you where the argument is weakest.

### Philosophy | Valid vs sound
**In short:** Valid means the conclusion follows from the premises. Sound means it's valid *and* the premises are true.
**Explained:** Validity is about the shape of an argument, not the facts. Ask: if the premises were true, would the conclusion have to be true? If yes, it's valid, even if the premises are nonsense. Soundness adds the reality check. Only a sound argument guarantees a true conclusion.
**Example:** "All fish can fly. A salmon is a fish. So salmon can fly." Valid, but the first premise is false, so it's unsound. The flip side: "Some politicians lie. Bob is a politician. So Bob lies." The premises may well be true, but the conclusion doesn't follow. Invalid.
**Why it matters:** Arguments fail in two ways: bad logic or bad facts. Check both before you accept the conclusion.

### Economics | Scarcity
**In short:** Resources are limited but wants aren't, so choices have to be made.
**Explained:** Scarcity doesn't mean something is rare. It means there isn't enough for everyone to have as much as they want for free. Time, money, land, workers and attention are all scarce. So every person, business and government has to decide what to do, what to give up, and who gets what. Economics is basically the study of those choices.
**Example:** A hospital has a fixed budget and a limited number of doctors. Opening a new cancer ward might mean longer waits in the emergency room. Neither option is "free", even if the patient never sees a bill.
**Why it matters:** When a politician promises something with no downside, scarcity is the question to ask: what gets less so this can get more?

### Economics | Opportunity cost
**In short:** The real cost of something is the best alternative you gave up to get it.
**Explained:** Costs aren't only money. Time, effort and missed chances count too. Economists look at the *next best* option you didn't take, because that's what the choice really cost you. Two people can pay the same price for something and face very different opportunity costs.
**Example:** University costs tuition, but also the wages you could have earned working for those 3–4 years. For most people it's still worth it. For a talented young footballer, or a teenager whose business is taking off, the hidden cost can be huge, which is why some sensibly skip it.
**Why it matters:** Price tags hide the biggest costs. Asking "what am I giving up?" makes decisions, personal and political, much clearer.

### Politics | Power
**In short:** Power is the ability to get others to do what they otherwise wouldn't.
**Explained:** Power comes in several forms. Coercion uses force or threats. Reward uses money, jobs or favours. Authority works when people accept that someone has the *right* to give orders. The political theorist Steven Lukes added a quieter kind: shaping what people want, or think is even possible, so they never object in the first place.
**Example:** A boss can order you to stay late (authority), pay you overtime (reward), threaten your job (coercion), or build a culture where staying late is "just what committed people do" (shaping beliefs). The last one doesn't need an order at all.
**Why it matters:** Politics is largely about who holds power, how they got it, and whether they use it fairly. The quiet kind is the hardest to notice, and the most useful to spot.

### Politics | The state
**In short:** The state is the permanent machinery that makes and enforces rules over a territory.
**Explained:** The state includes the government, parliament, courts, police, army and civil service. It's different from *the government*, which is just the group running it at the moment. The sociologist Max Weber defined the state as the organisation that claims the only *legitimate* right to use force in a territory. Others can use force, but only the state's use is accepted as rightful.
**Example:** After an election the government changes, but the tax office, courts and army carry on. And if a private company locks you in a room, that's kidnapping; if the police detain you under the law, it's legitimate.
**Why it matters:** Arguments about "big" versus "small" government are really arguments about how much the state should do.

## Day II

### Philosophy | Deductive vs inductive reasoning
**In short:** Deduction goes from general rules to a certain conclusion; induction goes from observed cases to a likely one.
**Explained:** In a good deductive argument, if the premises are true the conclusion *must* be true. Induction goes beyond the evidence: it spots a pattern and predicts it will continue. That makes it how we learn about the world, but it's never 100% certain. Science mostly runs on induction, then keeps testing it.
**Example:** Deduction: "Every employee gets a payslip. Sam is an employee. So Sam gets a payslip." Induction: "Every swan I've ever seen is white, so all swans are white." Europeans believed exactly that for centuries, until black swans were found in Australia.
**Why it matters:** When someone says something is "proven", ask which kind. Deductive proof is airtight; inductive evidence is only strong or weak, and one new case can overturn it.

### Philosophy | Ad hominem
**In short:** Attacking the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.
**Explained:** Latin for "to the person". It's tempting because it's easy and feels like a win, but it says nothing about whether the claim is true. A bad person can make a good argument, and a good person a bad one. Pointing out a real conflict of interest is fair, but even that is a reason to check more carefully, not proof the argument is wrong.
**Example:** "Why listen to her views on tax? She failed maths at school." Her grades from 20 years ago say nothing about whether her tax plan works. Compare: "The study saying sugar is harmless was paid for by a soft-drink company." Worth knowing, and worth checking the study closely, but it doesn't make the study false by itself.
**Why it matters:** When a debate turns personal, it's often a sign someone has run out of arguments.

### Economics | Incentives
**In short:** Incentives are rewards and penalties that change what people choose to do.
**Explained:** People respond to costs and benefits: money, time, status, guilt or convenience. Change the incentives and behaviour usually changes, often in ways the rule-makers didn't expect. A badly designed incentive that rewards the very thing you wanted less of is called a "perverse incentive".
**Example:** In colonial India, officials in Delhi are said to have paid a bounty for dead cobras to cut their numbers. People started breeding cobras for the reward. When the scheme was scrapped, the breeders set their snakes free, leaving more cobras than before. The story may be partly legend, but the "cobra effect" happens all the time in real policies.
**Why it matters:** Before judging any rule, ask: "What will people actually do because of this?"

### Economics | Thinking at the margin
**In short:** Most real decisions are about a little more or a little less, not all or nothing.
**Explained:** Economists call the extra benefit of one more unit the *marginal benefit*, and the extra cost the *marginal cost*. A sensible rule: keep going while the extra benefit is bigger than the extra cost, and stop when it isn't. The value of each extra unit usually falls; the first slice of pizza is great, the fifth much less so.
**Example:** A café that closes at 9 pm wonders about staying open until 10. The question isn't "is the café profitable?" but "will that one extra hour bring in more than it costs in wages, electricity and tired staff?" The café can be profitable overall and still lose money on that last hour.
**Why it matters:** "Is one more worth it?" is usually a better question than "Is this good?"

### Economics | Sunk costs
**In short:** A sunk cost is money, time or effort already spent that you can't get back.
**Explained:** Because it's gone whatever you decide next, it shouldn't affect your next decision; only future costs and benefits should. But people hate "wasting" what they've put in, so they stick with bad choices. That's the sunk cost fallacy.
**Example:** You bought a concert ticket, but on the night you feel ill. The money is spent either way, so the only real question is: "Would I rather be at the concert or at home right now?" Britain and France kept funding the supersonic Concorde long after it was clearly a money-loser, partly because so much had already been spent. It's even called the "Concorde fallacy".
**Why it matters:** "We've come too far to stop now" is one of the most expensive sentences in business and politics.

### Politics | Authority
**In short:** Authority is power that people accept as rightful. They obey because they think they should, not just because they must.
**Explained:** A mugger has power over you; a police officer has authority. The difference isn't strength but acceptance. Authority is much cheaper and more stable than force: if most people follow the rules willingly, you don't need a soldier on every corner. It can come from an official role, from expertise, or from personal respect.
**Example:** A referee is usually smaller than the players, yet a player shown a red card walks off. That's authority. When players and fans stop accepting the referee's decisions, a match turns chaotic fast. The whistle only works because people believe in it.
**Why it matters:** Governments rarely fall because they run out of weapons. They fall when people stop accepting their authority.

### Politics | Legitimacy
**In short:** Legitimacy is the widely shared belief that someone has the right to rule.
**Explained:** Max Weber described three sources. *Traditional*: "it has always been this way", like hereditary kings. *Charismatic*: loyalty to an extraordinary leader. *Legal-rational*: power that comes from agreed rules, like elections and constitutions. Most modern states rely on the third, often with a bit of the other two mixed in.
**Example:** The British monarchy keeps traditional legitimacy even though it has little real power. Revolutionary leaders like Fidel Castro leaned heavily on charisma. A newly elected prime minister gets legitimacy from winning under agreed rules, which is why disputed elections are so dangerous: if people doubt the rules, they doubt the winner's right to rule.
**Why it matters:** Even dictators hold elections. They're chasing legitimacy, because ruling by force alone is expensive and fragile.

## Day III

### Philosophy | Straw man
**In short:** Twisting someone's argument into a weaker, exaggerated version, then knocking that down instead.
**Explained:** The name comes from a scarecrow: easy to beat because it can't fight back. It's popular because it makes you look like you've won without dealing with what the other person actually said. It often starts with "So what you're saying is…"
**Example:** A: "I think we should spend a bit less on the military and a bit more on schools." B: "So you want to leave the country defenceless?" A never said that. B is attacking an extreme position that's far easier to beat than the real one.
**Why it matters:** A good test in any debate: could the other side read your summary of their view and say "yes, that's what I mean"? If not, you might be arguing with a scarecrow.

### Philosophy | Appeal to authority
**In short:** Claiming something is true just because an important or famous person said so.
**Explained:** Trusting experts is often sensible; nobody can check everything themselves. It becomes a fallacy when the "authority" isn't an expert in that area, when real experts widely disagree, or when someone's status is used to shut down the evidence. The useful question isn't "who said it?" but "how would they know?"
**Example:** "A Nobel Prize-winning physicist says this diet cures cancer." Being brilliant at physics doesn't make someone an expert on nutrition. Compare: "The large majority of climate scientists agree the planet is warming." That's a reasonable appeal: relevant experts, broad agreement, and evidence you could check.
**Why it matters:** Advertising runs on this fallacy. A famous athlete selling a supplement, or an actor in a white coat, isn't evidence.

### Economics | Demand
**In short:** Demand is how much of something people are willing *and able* to buy at different prices.
**Explained:** The law of demand says that, other things being equal, when the price rises people buy less, and when it falls they buy more. "Able" matters: wanting a sports car isn't demand if you can't pay for it. Demand as a whole can also shift when tastes, incomes or the prices of related goods change.
**Example:** When a supermarket halves the price of strawberries and they sell out, that's a price effect. When a viral video makes a recipe famous and people buy more of an ingredient at the *same* price, that's demand itself shifting.
**Why it matters:** Sales can rise because something got cheaper or because people want it more. Knowing which one helps you read headlines about "booming" or "collapsing" markets.

### Economics | Supply
**In short:** Supply is how much of something producers are willing and able to sell at different prices.
**Explained:** Usually, the higher the price, the more producers want to sell, because it's more profitable and worth the extra effort. Supply as a whole shifts when production costs, technology, weather or the number of sellers change. Where supply meets demand, you get the market price.
**Example:** When coffee prices rise, farmers plant more coffee, and some switch over from other crops. But a frost in Brazil, the world's biggest coffee grower, can wipe out part of a harvest, cutting supply and pushing prices up worldwide, even though nobody's taste for coffee changed.
**Why it matters:** When prices jump, ask: did people want more, or could sellers offer less? The answer points to very different fixes.

### Politics | Sovereignty
**In short:** Sovereignty is supreme authority over a territory: no higher power inside it, and no outside power above it.
**Explained:** The modern idea is often traced to the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which ended decades of religious war in Europe with the principle that states shouldn't meddle in each other's internal affairs. Sovereignty has an internal side (the final say over laws at home) and an external side (being recognised as independent by other states).
**Example:** A country setting its own tax rates is exercising sovereignty. Joining the EU meant pooling some of it, for example by following shared trade rules. That's why the Brexit campaign's slogan was "Take back control."
**Why it matters:** Arguments about trade deals, international courts or foreign intervention are usually arguments about how much sovereignty a country should keep or share.

### Politics | Nation vs state
**In short:** A state is a political organisation with borders and a government. A nation is a group of people who feel they share an identity.
**Explained:** The two often overlap, giving us the "nation-state", but not always. A nation can exist without its own state, and one state can contain several nations. National identity can rest on language, history, religion, culture, or simply a shared story people believe in.
**Example:** The Kurds are a nation of roughly 30–40 million people spread across Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Syria, with no state of their own. The United Kingdom is one state containing several nations: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, which even play as separate football teams.
**Why it matters:** Many conflicts, from independence movements to civil wars, come from a mismatch between nations and state borders.

### Politics | Rule of law
**In short:** The rule of law means everyone, including those in power, must follow the same known laws, applied fairly.
**Explained:** It rests on a few ideas: laws are public and clear, they apply equally to everyone, courts are independent, and nobody can be punished except under the law. It's different from "rule *by* law", where rulers use laws as a weapon against others while staying above them.
**Example:** In South Korea, President Park Geun-hye was impeached in 2017, removed from office and later jailed for corruption. A sitting leader being held to the same law as everyone else is the rule of law working. In countries where courts never rule against the government, laws exist, but the rule of law doesn't.
**Why it matters:** Without the rule of law, rights on paper mean little, and business, investment and everyday trust all suffer.
